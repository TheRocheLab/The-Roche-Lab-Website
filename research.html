Our Research

Exploring the molecular mechanisms of synaptic function through genetics, imaging, and electrophysiological approaches

The Question(s)

The human brain depends on billions of connections between neurons called synapses. These connections allow us to move, learn, remember, and adapt to the world around us. When synapses do not form correctly or fail to function properly, the consequences can range from developmental disorders to neurodegenerative disease.

At the Roche Lab, we are interested in understanding how synapses are built, maintained, and modified over time. We study the proteins and molecular pathways that allow neurons to communicate with one another, focusing on how these systems work together to support healthy synaptic function and plasticity.

By investigating how proteins regulate vesicle trafficking, organize synaptic structure, and coordinate signaling at the synapse, we hope to uncover the fundamental mechanisms that enable neural communication. Ultimately, our goal is to better understand how these processes break down in disease and what that can teach us about protecting and preserving nervous system function.

The Approach

Answering questions about how synapses develop and function requires a model system that is both accessible and genetically tractable. In the Roche Lab, we use the Drosophila neuromuscular junction (NMJ), a widely used model for studying synaptic biology.

What makes Drosophila especially valuable is that many of the molecular mechanisms that govern synaptic function are remarkably conserved across species. The NMJ shares key features with vertebrate glutamatergic synapses, allowing us to study fundamental biological processes in a genetically accessible system.

To connect molecular mechanisms with biological function, we combine genetics, high-resolution imaging, and electrophysiology. These approaches allow us to manipulate specific genes, visualize synaptic structure and protein localization, and measure how effectively neurons communicate with their targets.

By bringing these tools together, we can move beyond identifying individual molecules and begin to understand how they work together to build, maintain, and adapt synaptic connections. Ultimately, this helps us uncover the fundamental principles that govern nervous system development and function.

Current Projects & Mechanisms

Role of the exocyst complex in synaptic development and function

We combine imaging techniques and electrophysiology to investigate how the exocyst complex regulates synaptic development and function at the Drosophila neuromuscular junction. We have isolated Sec15, a component of the exocyst complex involved in targeted vesicle exocytosis, and found that loss of this protein leads to a range of developmental and functional deficits at the NMJ.

Many of these phenotypes have been previously characterized (Kang et al., 2024), and we remain interested in how this protein complex functions at neuronal synapses. In particular, we are currently focused on how the exocyst complex contributes to synaptic transmission.

Par1 kinase regulation of Dlg localization in synaptic development

We investigate the interaction between Par1 kinase and Dlg, a scaffolding protein homologous to mammalian PSD-95, in neuronal development and function. These proteins were identified in a genetic screen for regulators of synapse development.

We are particularly interested in how Par1 kinase regulates the localization of Dlg and how this, in turn, affects the localization and function of other synaptic proteins organized by Dlg.

Effects of CaMKII and UBE3A Variant Expression at the Drosophila NMJ

CaMKII and UBE3A are two proteins that are important for neurodevelopment and synaptic plasticity, with changes to expression or function leading to great disruptions to synapses and synaptic transmission. Cell culture studies by the UMass Amherst Strieter Lab revealed that UBE3A-mediated degradation of CaMKII mutants occurred to a significantly higher degree than that of wild-type CaMKII.

This project attempted to understand the in vivo interactions between UBE3A and CaMKII variants using the Drosophila NMJ as a model for vertebrate glutamatergic synapses. In particular, we wished to determine whether co-overexpression of the Drosophila UBE3A homolog with mutant CaMKII homologs would rescue the effects of CaMKII overexpression, as characterized in initial third-instar NMJ morphology and organismal viability analyses of individual variant overexpressions.
